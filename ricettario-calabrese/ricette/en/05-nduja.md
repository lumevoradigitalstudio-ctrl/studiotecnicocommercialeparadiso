# 'Nduja (spreadable spicy pork salume)

*'Nduja · Vibo Valentia, Spilinga and Monte Poro · winter*

In the villages of Monte Poro, 'nduja is made in winter, when the cold helps the meat and the family gathers around the pig. It is a soft, dark red salume that you spread with a knife and that melts in a hot pan. It is made from the fatty parts of the pig, ground very fine and bound together by a great deal of chilli. Every village has its own level of heat, and every family its own mix of sweet and hot chilli. This page offers two routes. The first is fresh fridge 'nduja, simple and always eaten cooked. The second is cured 'nduja, the kind you spread on bread, which needs equipment and patience.

**Makes:** about 1.4 kg (3.1 lb) cured 'nduja (from 2 kg (4.4 lb) of raw mixture) · **Prep:** 2 hours · **Cook:** none · **Rest:** 48 hours + 4-8 weeks · **Total:** about 2 months · **Difficulty:** challenging

## Before you start: safety

A raw salume cured at home is safe only if you follow every rule below. If you are missing even one piece of equipment, make the fresh fridge 'nduja instead (variation further down). The general rules are at the beginning of this chapter.

- **Inspected meat.** Buy pork from a butcher, from pigs slaughtered under veterinary inspection for *Trichinella*.
- **Cold and cleanliness.** Keep meat and equipment at 0-4 °C (32-39 °F) until you work them.
- **Curing salt.** Nitrite is needed against botulism, at the legal dose (see the start of the chapter). Chilli alone is not enough.
- **Measuring tools.** A scale accurate to 1 g, a thermo-hygrometer for the curing space, and a food pH meter with strips or probe.

## Ingredients

**For 2 kg (4.4 lb) of mixture**
- 1 kg (2.2 lb) firm pork fat, from the jowl or back
- 400 g (14 oz) fresh skinless pork belly
- 600 g (1.3 lb) lean pork shoulder
- 200 to 300 g (11 oz) ground Calabrian chilli, half sweet and half hot
- curing salt, calculated as at the start of the chapter, limit 80 mg/kg; with EU curing salt at 0.6% that is about 26 g (0.9 oz)
- fine sea salt, to bring the total salt to 56 g (2 oz), that is 2.8%
- 0.5 g starter culture for fermented sausages, or the dose on the pack
- natural beef or wide hog casing (bung or caecum), about 2 m (7 ft), cleaned

## Method

1. Put the fat, belly and lean meat in the freezer for 30-40 minutes, until firm at the edges but not frozen.
2. Cut everything into 3 cm (1¼ in) cubes. Chill the grinder parts in the fridge.
3. Weigh the curing salt, sea salt and culture. Dissolve the culture in a tablespoon of cold, unchlorinated water.
4. Grind everything with the 8 mm (⅜ in) plate. Put the mixture back in the fridge for 20 minutes.
5. Add the chilli, salts and culture. Knead with your hands for 4-5 minutes, until the mixture is sticky and evenly red.
6. Grind again with the 4 mm (⅛ in) plate. For a finer 'nduja, grind a third time with the 3 mm (⅛ in) plate. The mixture should look like a thick paste, with no visible pieces of fat.
7. Fry a teaspoon of the mixture and taste it. Adjust only the chilli, never the curing salt.
8. Soak the casing in lukewarm water for 30 minutes and rinse it inside too.
9. Stuff the mixture in tightly, with no air pockets. Prick any remaining bubbles with a clean needle.
10. Tie each piece tightly with twine. Weigh each 'nduja and write the weight on a tag.
11. Ferment: hang the 'nduje for 48 hours at 20-24 °C (68-75 °F) with humidity above 90%, for example in a closed room with a bowl of hot water.
12. Measure the pH after 48 hours. It must be 5.3 or lower. If it is higher, wait 24 hours and measure again. If it has not dropped after 72 hours, throw the mixture away.
13. If you want to smoke them, do it now: cold smoke (below 25 °C (77 °F)) from oak or olive wood, 6-8 hours over several days. Smoking is optional.
14. Cure the 'nduje at 12-15 °C (54-59 °F) with 75-85% humidity, in a dark, ventilated space.
15. Weigh them every week. They are ready when they have lost 25-30% of their starting weight, usually after 4-8 weeks. They should still feel soft but compact.
16. Cut the first 'nduja. Inside it should be dark red and even, with a good smell of chilli and cured meat.

## Easier variation: fresh fridge 'nduja

Follow steps 1 to 7, then pack the mixture firmly into clean jars, or stuff it into casings. Keep it in the fridge at 0-4 °C (32-39 °F) and eat it **within 2 weeks, always cooked**: in a sauce, on pizza, in a pan. Don't spread it raw.

## Tips
- The fat must stay cold: if it warms up, it smears and separates. Work in a cool room.
- Use well-dried chilli from the current season. Damp chilli brings mould.
- Dry white mould on the casing is normal. Wipe it off with a cloth dipped in vinegar.

## Common mistakes
- Don't cure 'nduja in a cellar that is too warm or too damp: above 18 °C (64 °F) it ferments too much, above 90% humidity it goes mouldy.
- Don't leave out the curing salt because "it wasn't used in the old days". The old recipe was made in the winter cold, with risks that aren't worth taking today.
- Don't dry it too much: beyond 35% weight loss it turns hard and won't spread.

## Local variations
- Families vary the fatty part: some use more jowl, others more belly.
- The heat changes from village to village, by playing with the share of sweet and hot chilli.

## Storage
- Whole cured 'nduja: in a cool place at 12-15 °C (54-59 °F) or in the fridge for a few months.
- Once cut: cover the cut end with cling film and keep it in the fridge, eating it within 2-3 weeks.

## Substitutions
- If you can't find ground Calabrian chilli, use equal parts sweet unsmoked paprika and cayenne pepper.
- Natural casings are sold by butchers and online sausage-making shops.
- Curing salt and starter cultures are sold by meat-processing suppliers.

## Allergens (14 EU allergens)
None in the basic ingredients. Check the labels of starter cultures and spice blends, which sometimes contain milk or mustard.

## PDO and PGI
'Nduja has neither PDO nor PGI.

## Wine
A full-bodied Calabrian red, such as a **Cirò Rosso DOC**.
